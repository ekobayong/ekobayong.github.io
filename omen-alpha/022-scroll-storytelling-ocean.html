Zone 01 · 0–200 m

Epipelagic

The sunlit zone holds more than ninety percent of all marine life, and for good reason: this is the only layer where enough light survives for photosynthesis. Drifting phytoplankton here fix carbon on a scale comparable to all the land forests combined, feeding everything from sardine shoals to oceanic turtles riding the current.

Colour is the first casualty of depth. Red light is absorbed within the first few metres, orange by thirty, and by the bottom of the zone the world has narrowed to a cold, luminous blue. Fish that live here tend to be countershaded — dark above, silver below — vanishing against either surface or sky.

Zone 02 · 200–1,000 m

Mesopelagic

The twilight zone is where daylight finally loses its argument. Vision here is a currency: eyes grow enormous, and between 500 and 800 metres the lanternfish stage the largest migration on Earth, rising nightly by the billion to feed near the surface and sinking again before dawn.

It is also the realm of living light. Roughly three quarters of twilight-zone animals make their own glow — photophores tuned to the exact blue that seawater transmits best, used for lures, signals, and counter-illumination cloaks that erase their own silhouette.

Zone 03 · 1,000–4,000 m

Bathypelagic

Below a kilometre no sunlight has ever arrived, not once, in the history of the ocean. The midnight zone runs on scraps: marine snow, the slow fall of everything that dies above. A sperm whale hunting here is the largest predator ever to use sound as sight, broadcasting clicks of over two hundred decibels into the black.

The anglerfish has taken patience to its logical extreme — a lure of cultivated bacteria, a body built to wait weeks between meals. Food is so rare that when it appears, the deep eats it where it falls; whole communities assemble around a single sunken whale for decades.

Zone 04 · 4,000–6,000 m

Abyssopelagic

The abyss is the planet's default landscape: rolling plains of sediment laid down a fingernail's thickness per millennium, covering more than half the Earth. Life is sparse, small, and slow — a fish here may go months between meals and move at the pace of drifting dust.

What the abyss lacks in energy it makes up in constancy. Temperature barely varies from two degrees; salinity is set like a metronome. Creatures such as the crimson Atolla jelly drift with the patience of the rocks beneath them, burning nets of blue light when touched.

Zone 05 · 6,000–10,935 m

Hadal

Named for Hades, the hadal zone exists only in trenches, where one tectonic plate dives beneath another and drags the seafloor down with it. The Challenger Deep bottoms out at 10,935 metres — Mount Everest would vanish into it with two kilometres of water to spare.

And still it is inhabited. Pale snailfish, soft and translucent, drift above the trench floor at pressures a thousand times greater than at the surface. Amphipods graze the silt. The hadal is the least explored habitat on Earth, and by every measure the largest reservation of true wilderness remaining.

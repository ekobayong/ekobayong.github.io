From the sunlit surface of the ocean down to the Challenger Deep at 10,935 metres, the water becomes a second sky — then a night that never ends.

0 – 200 m

Epipelagic

This is the photic zone, a thin film of the planet where sunlight still paints the water turquoise. Photosynthesis runs the open-ocean food web here: phytoplankton bloom, sardines braid the light, and turtles graze the living weather of the surface.

Ninety percent of all marine life lives in this first two hundred metres. Below it, the sun becomes a rumour.

200 – 1,000 m

Mesopelagic

Twilight. Blue drains to indigo. Eyes grow large, and bodies grow lamps. Lanternfish rise at night to feed in the shallows, then fall again — the largest animal migration on Earth, performed in darkness.

Jellyfish pulse like slow planets. The last photons die around a thousand metres; after that, every light is homemade.

1,000 – 4,000 m

Bathypelagic

Midnight water. Sperm whales hunt squid in a night without weather. Anglerfish carry a bacterial sun on a fishing rod, a private star in a universe of black.

Pressure here would crush a submarine’s older cousins. Life answers with jelly, oil, and patience. Nothing hurries.

4,000 – 6,000 m

Abyssopelagic

The abyss is a desert of snow: marine snow, the slow rain of the dead from above. Atolla jellies flare crimson when startled — a burglar alarm meant for larger mouths.

The seafloor, when it comes, is plains of clay and the occasional carcass that becomes an oasis for years.

6,000 – 10,935 m

Hadal

Trenches fold the crust until the ocean has a basement. In the Challenger Deep, snailfish — pale, scaleless, almost translucent — graze bacterial films at pressures above a thousand atmospheres.

We have spent less time here than on the Moon. The maps still have rooms without names.

Method

Two hypothetical chemicals, A and B, diffuse across the glass at unequal rates — the substrate at one, the activator at half. Where B is present it consumes A and reproduces itself; A is secretly fed back at rate f, and B dies at rate k. The classic three-by-three Laplacian does the bookkeeping: centre minus one, orthogonal neighbours at point two, diagonals at point zero five, wrapped toroidally so the specimen has no edges and no escape.

Tweak f and k by a few ten-thousandths and the same chemistry yields coral branches, dividing cells, slugs, leopard spots, or a maze. Nothing here is stored — the pattern is a standing wave of process, and the glass must be kept fed or the image dies within a minute.

Handling

Hold the slide by the charcoal mount. Inoculate with the brush only along the pointer path; the culture closes small wounds into new organs within seconds, so draw slowly for coral and quickly for lace. Never flood the whole glass — an over-dosed field of B burns itself out and leaves the substrate barren.

If the specimen fails to organise after a parameter change, reseed: seven discs at random plus one at centre. To fix a stage for study, press Photograph; a 1600-cell plate with catalogue caption will be issued and the glass may be returned to the incubator.

Provenance

The model descends from Alan Turing's 1952 paper on the chemical basis of morphogenesis, and was given its working form by Pearson in 1991, whose atlas of f/k space — spots, worms, mitosis, coral, maze — is still the taxonomist's map of these cultures. Our five catalogue presets are lifted honestly from that map.

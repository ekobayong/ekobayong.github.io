Monday 15 September — low tide at first light

Went out before the mist had made up its mind. The sandbar lay open like a country no one had claimed, ribbed and silver, and I walked it nearly to the buoy.

Sea kale in flower on the dune crest, four or five heads of it, lilac and perfect; I did not pick any. I said that aloud, to no one, the way one does.

Stone-skimming, a lost art I took up again: seven skips off the flat grey one, six off the white. The arm remembers what the ledger forgets.

— Gull overhead, mouth open, disagreeing with everything.

Turned back as the light came up the east ridge. The wreck stood in a pool of gold, and I understood, briefly and completely, why I keep coming.

the sandbar, 6:52 a.m.

Things to remember:

  • Seals lie on a bank the way commas sit in a sentence — the pause is the meaning.
  • Every harbour keeps one wreck as a promise.
  • Curlew before sunrise, always.
  • Ask the detector-man about the bayonet.
  • Do not pick the sea kale.

Seven skips. I can do seven skips.

Bought stamps in the village. The tide tables page is stuck to the inside cover with the lavender in it; next month's go in the back pocket, with the receipts and the one good photograph.

Saturday 13 September — the saltmarsh path

Took the inland way this time, over the plank bridge where the water was still brackish-green and moving. The marsh between the creeks is one long breathing thing: cordgrass, samphire, the black skeletons of last year's reeds.

Redshank at every puddle, lifting one leg then the other, theatrical little sentinels. Two curlew on the high bank worked the mud with those impossible bills, and their calls came over the marsh the way church bells come over a town — the same sound, but belonging to the whole place.

Sampled seed heads, one thrift plant still in flower:

Cut back to the sea wall at the old beacon. The tide was on the turn and the spit had shrunk to a ribbon.

Pencil: plank bridge rattles worse — report it? No. Let it rattle.

Up the spit the long way as the tide began its business. The seals moved onto the sand bank ahead of me in twos and threes and one fat solitary, settling with a wriggle, and I stopped counting because ten was enough. The old warden's phrase came back: they lie on a bank like commas on a line. I have never improved on it.

The detector-man was out again, headphones like a second skull, sweeping his coil with the patience of a saint and the luck of a fisherman. His bucket holds, he tells me: a field of buttons, a bayonet, forty-odd bottle caps, and one Georgian coin he will never spend.

Pressed the sea lavender over the October tables, here:

Friday 12 September — the harbour in mist

Left the gate at six past, when the harbour was still asleep under a lid of mist and the only sound was the mooring chains worrying about it. The water was hammered tin. You could smell the mud flats from the quay — good and ancient.

Two egrets standing on the old breakwater like heron drawn by someone who had only heard about heron. They let me come within ten paces, then went up without hurry, feet trailing, white swallowed by white until the wings were all I had.

Oystercatchers on the slipway, piping that thin, electric peep that is the sound this coast makes at dawn.

And there it was, as it is every September: the wreck on the shingle spit, ribs showing, name-board long since gone to worm and salt. The plaque at the harbour says Kestrel, built at Yarmouth, out of Plymouth. It does not say what she ran afoul of. I think it was mist like this.

Mist out by seven. Bread in the satchel. Tea in the flask, gone cold before I wanted it.

Walked the quay road east at half past and did not go in at the place where the shingle begins, because that is a walk for the afternoon, and I meant to earn it. The creek path has dropped its summer crowd; the benches hold nothing but mist and one newspaper, folded to the racing.

Counted on the way back, from the high gate: six seals on the inner bank, eleven in the water as heads, and one — a great cow, obviously — hauled out above the tide line with the gracelessness of a spilled sack, snoring.

I stood there a long while. The mist thinned enough to throw the sun's one coin on the water and be done with it.

Pencil: the warden's son says the seals "commas" remark was his father's, and his father's before that. Good line, family. Good line.

Ebb & flow — the last page

Tide tables for October pasted in and checked against the harbour board; the lavender is on them, under the plank bridge notes, with the one good photograph gone out of the back pocket and into a frame.

The walk begins again at the next spring tides, if the spit is still there, and if I am.

— E. H.

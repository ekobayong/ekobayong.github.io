The press room: how the dots make the picture

Halftone screens. A continuous-tone image can't be printed with one flat ink — so each colour layer is chopped into a grid of dots. Dense areas get fat dots, bright areas get pinheads. The four process plates run at slightly different angles (cyan 0°, magenta 60°, yellow −15°, black 30°) so their lattices never stack up into a moiré — the slider rotates the whole family together.

Ben-Day fills. Comics printers like Roy Ben-Day made flat colour cheap: instead of ink coverage, they used evenly spaced dots of a single hue over paper. Squint and your eye averages them back into a tone. Our page background is the same trick — radial-gradient dots on a 14 px grid.

Comic outlines. A Sobel operator convolves the luminance buffer to find sharp edges, then the edge map is stamped several times with sub-pixel offsets — dilation — which is why the “Outline weight” slider turns hairline art into inky brush. Multiply compositing stacks the plates the way an offset press does.

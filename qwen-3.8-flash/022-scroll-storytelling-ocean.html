Descent

A single continuous dive from the sunlit surface of the sea to the floor of the Challenger Deep — 10,935 metres below. The ocean keeps everything below the waterline in darkness; nearly all of it, in cold.

Zone I · 0 – 200 m

Epipelagic — the sunlight zone

This thin lens of lit water is the ocean's whole engine room. Enough light reaches the top two hundred metres for photosynthesis, and almost every food web in the sea — down to the last hadal amphipod — runs on the energy fixed here, by phytoplankton that together produce roughly half the oxygen you will ever breathe.

It is also the warmest and most crowded layer: tuna, turtles, mantas and sardine schools live their entire lives within it. Schools flash and fold like weather; a hawksbill turtle will spend eighty years never knowing a deeper place than this.

Zone II · 200 – 1,000 m

Mesopelagic — the twilight zone

Below the last useful photon, colour drains away: red light is gone by fifteen metres, orange by forty, and everything that remains is blue. The twilight zone is lit instead by animals — nearly every creature here makes its own light, from rows of photophores along a lanternfish's belly to the counter-illuminations that erase a silhouette against the faint glow above.

Every night this zone empties upward: billions of tonnes of fish, squid and krill rise to feed under darkness in the diel vertical migration, the largest coordinated movement of animals on the planet — and one of the largest carbon transfers, pumping carbon to the deep like a slow biological conveyor.

Zone III · 1,000 – 4,000 m

Bathypelagic — the midnight zone

Total darkness, near-freezing water, and a food economy based entirely on rain: the dead things, scales, mucus and pellets that fall from the sunlit world above, nicknamed marine snow. An anglerfish waits in it, dangling a bulb of bacterial light; a sperm whale dives through it, hunting giant squid by sonar in pressure that crushes air spaces to nothing.

About every few decades a whale dies in this zone and lands on the abyssal floor below — a whale fall that can feed an entire local ecosystem for half a century, its own sun in the dark.

Zone IV · 4,000 – 6,000 m

Abyssopelagic — the abyss

The abyssal plains cover more than half the planet's surface and are its least explored terrain. The water here is a uniform, motionless 2–3 °C — the deep ocean's common room, stirred only by slow currents older than the ice ages. An Atolla jelly rings itself with a burgundy alarm display: when attacked, it fires a wheel of bioluminescence to attract something bigger to eat the attacker.

On the floor below, sea cucumbers walk the sediment like vacuum cleaners, tripod fish stand on stilted fins facing the current, and life is sparse, patient, and evenly distributed — everywhere, barely.

Zone V · 6,000 – 10,935 m

Hadal — the trenches

Named for Hades, the hadal zone exists only in the ocean's subduction trenches — narrow scars where one plate slides beneath another. It is a world of steep walls and freezing sediment. In 2017 a translucent, smiling snailfish was filmed at 8,336 metres in the Izu-Ogasawara Trench, breaking its own record as the deepest fish ever seen; snailfish have been found beyond 8,000 m in five separate trench systems.

At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the water column presses down at over a thousand atmospheres — the weight of a hundred elephants standing on a postage stamp — and life persists anyway: amphipods swarm a fallen whale bone in minutes, and the sediment teems with foraminifera the size of fists.

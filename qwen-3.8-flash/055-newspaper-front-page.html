Late Edition

Airship Meridian Spans Ocean in Sixty-One Hours

Twelve Hundred Souls Hailed at Dawn as Keel Reached Harbor Behind Breakwater — Wireless Operator Sends Single Word: “Arrived”
By Our Aerial Correspondent, Halvard Quill

The dirigible Meridian, silver as a wet knife against the morning, came down through a screen of cloud at 6:12 today and anchored above the Municipal Masted with her bow toward home. Sixty-one hours out of Oldport she crossed the rim of our harbor bar — nine hours ahead of the posted schedule, and with, her captain said, “a little gas and a great deal of luck.”

Crowds had begun to walk seaward before the first newsboys had finished the second edition’s cry. By four o’clock in the morning the esplanade was a black ribbon of hats, and when the searchers from the north point strobed the fog at half past five the cheering ran along the whole water front like a fired fuse.

Captain Ilse Brandt, speaking from the control car with oil yet on her cuffs, said the Atlantic had been “nearly rude.” A squall off the banks at mid-passage tore away one stabilizing vane and wet the wireless room through a cracked port, but the hull was never in doubt. Chief engineer Marlowe reported all eight screws turning true and the gas plant steady through the worst of it.

Aboard were twelve hundred passengers, among them the Chinese mission of Doctor Shen, six nuns bound for the seaside convent at Ann’s Point, a traveling menagerie missing exactly one rhea, and the Gazette’s own correspondent, whose letters, filed by wireless hour by hour, made this the best-reported crossing ever made in any direction.

The mooring tower took the ship at 7:04. The gangplank was down at 7:31. It was, the harbor master said afterward, “the quietest thing that ever happened in this town.” By noon the streets were festival again, and the bands on the ferry steps had played nothing but the arrival march for an hour straight.

What the Meridian has proven, in sixty-one unarguable hours, is small enough to say on a card: the ocean has become a street, and this city stands at one of its corners.

Late Wires
— 7:41 A.M., CABLE HEAD —

Quake Rattles Coast Counties; No Damage Owned

A shock at four o’clock this morning set the windows ringing from Point Clear to Anse Bay and spilled the milk in half the kitchens of Marrow County. The observatory records twelve seconds of movement. No wall has fallen and no fire has followed, and by breakfast the road commission had opened every mountain pass except the high way at Silver Notch.

— 9:12 A.M., BY RADIO —

Premier Talks Three Hours, Says Nothing; Market Holds

Across the water the premier’s long conference with the union committees closed before noon without a word to the reporters waiting below. The exchanges, which had spent the morning frightened, spent the afternoon cheerful, and the cable companies refuse to sell a story they cannot spell.

Fire Eats Grand Hotel Roof; Guests Dined Unshod in Square

No Life Lost — Engine 4 Reached First Through the Service Alley

Flames found the Grand Hotel’s attic shortly after eleven last night, and by the time the bell at Fourth and Water had finished its argument with the neighborhood the whole roof was a bonnet of fire. The brigade played from the alley and the roof of the stable, and saved the marble front to the fourth floor.

Two hundred guests came down the service stair in dancing clothes and dinner jackets open at the throat, and the cafe across the square served them coffee until three without once raising the check. The manager, damp to the shoulders, said the hotel would honor every engagement. The cause is set down to the laundry flues, and the insurance men are already being polite to one another at the bar.

Radio Sends Symphony to Farm Homes; 40,000 Sets Heard Beethoven Before the Stove

Station WMC Plays an Hour Out of the Concert Hall — Receiver Makers Report a Run on Valves

From eight to nine last evening the Fifth moved through wire and ether into kitchens, parlors and one lighthouse, and our correspondent at the console says the studio was so quiet during the second movement that the players could hear the tower clock. The station announces a second broadcast Friday and warns owners to expect static with the change of weather.

Dealers on Merchants Row report such a run on receiving sets and valves that the window of the Columbia Electric parlors was dressed yesterday with a single horn and a card reading “In Demand — In Fact, Insanely.” Even our cousins on the stock ranges, it is said, heard the horns.

Sporting

Harbor Nines Trim College Club, 7 to 4, in Twelve-Inning Scuffle

The game hung fire nine innings and split open in the tenth, when Fife’s double to the gap and a wild throw to the plate ended an argument the benches had been rehearsing all afternoon. The league pennant now waits on Saturday’s double header at the grounds on Beacon Field, weather and the commissioner permitting.

Council Fights Speed Law Two Hours; Sets Limit at Twenty Where Children Play

Signs to Go Up First of the Month — Auto Club Warns of “A Trap of Paint”

After two hours of the aldermen talking about automobiles as if they were horses, the council by nine to four fixed twenty miles an hour wherever a school or playground opens on the street, and thirty elsewhere within the city line. The Auto Club’s counsel protested that a motorist cannot measure a schoolyard from the saddle.

The warden of schools asked that the limit be twenty wherever children are, “which,” she said, “is nearly everywhere the sun shines.” The fine for the first offense is five dollars, for the second fifteen, and the judges have been asked to remember that the point is not revenue.

Society

The Aldenballs give the autumn at-home this afternoon at the house on Crescent Way, the first since the return from the coast. The rooms were banked with fern and the fountain ran with rose water, and the violins played until five.

Mrs. V. K. Trowbridge announces the engagement of her daughter Harriet to Lieutenant Sam Okonkwo, lately of the airship service — the same, our society department is instructed to add with importance, who signaled the Meridian’s position by lamp from the bowsprit.

Harbor Board Reports Best Year Since the War: Ferry Passengers Up 400,000

Dredging of the South Channel Promised for the Spring — Wharfage Receipts Pay the Interest Clean

The board’s clerk read a statement that surprised even its friends: nine and a half million tons through the harbor, forty thousand more on the ferries than in 1925, and a sinking fund for the south channel within a single bond issue of done. The chairman thanked the pilots by name, which the minutes record as unmannerly and correct.

Against the good news the commissioners set one caution: the breakwater’s outer head wants stone before the winter gales, and the engineer was told to have the survey on their desk by Monday.

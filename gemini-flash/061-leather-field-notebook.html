Field Journal No. 3 · Autumn Migration & Coastal Drift
3rd September — Dawn at Blakeney Harbour

The dawn tide creeps in without a sound, filling the braided mud gutters until the grey harbour swells like a plate of beaten silver. A bitter tang of cold salt and bladderwrack hangs on the air. Through the dissolving sea-fret, three little egrets stand motionless in the samphire shallows—pure white daggers against the slate creek.

Suddenly the shrill, piping cry of oystercatchers cuts through the hush, six birds skittering low over the glassy water, black-and-white wings flickering, their orange bills bright as embers. Walked past the moored crab boats clinking against the wooden piles. The thermos lid is warm in my palms; black coffee and chicory.

Barometer 1018 hPa, wind NW F2. High water 06:40. Counted 14 egrets along the outer channel before mist lifted.
5th September — The Shingle Spit & Wreck

Trudging out along the shingle bank toward the western point, boots crunching on millions of rounded flints. Halfway along lies the broken spine of the Vanguard, the old timber collier grounded in the winter gale of 1912. Her black oak ribs rise from the stones like the skeleton of a whale, studded with rusted iron bolts and crusts of acorn barnacles.

Met Arthur sweeping the gravel with his battered detector, headphones crooked over his woollen cap. He grinned and tapped a pocket: ‘The February storms stripped four feet of shingle off the clay,’ he told me. Showed me a heavy cartwheel twopence he dug by the wreck—thick as a ship's biscuit, frosted with pale sea-verdigris.

Arthur's coin: George III, 1797. Pure copper. He says timber exposed this winter for the first time in thirty years.
7th September — Saltmarsh Spring Tides

Spring tides have flooded the lower mudflats, but the upper marsh is an unbroken sea of lavender. For miles the grey-green silt is drowned in a smoky violet mist—thousands of tiny papery florets trembling in the seaward breeze. I pressed a sprig between the leaves of the tide table yesterday; already the stems are stiff and papery, holding their pale amethyst hue.

Tide Tables · Wells & Blakeney Bar
HW 07:14 (7.4m) · LW 13:42 (1.1m) · HW 19:38 (7.2m)

The whole marsh hums with solitary bees taking the last nectar before the equinoctial floods drown the rootstocks. Crushed samphire smells of green brine and pepper.

Limonium vulgare · Sea Lavender. Retains color for seasons when dried flat.
9th September — The Outer Marshes & Waders

Stepped off the gravel track onto the sponge of the marsh. Without warning, a redshank bursts from a hidden creek with a frantic, piercing yelp—the sharp ‘tü-hü-hü’ that warns every living creature within half a mile. The true watchman of the coast.

Farther out on the vast sandflats, seven curlews probe the mud with their sickle-curved bills. Their call drifts over the water—that wild, liquid bubbling whistle that makes the throat tighten. It is the oldest voice on this coast, unchanged since the ice retreated.

Glaucium flavum · Yellow Horned Poppy found on shingle ridge near Stiffkey Freshes.
11th September — Low Tide & Sea Kale

Low water exposes half a league of rippled yellow sandbanks. Out on the spit, twenty harbour seals are hauled out in the warm sun. From this distance their curved resting bodies look exactly like commas arranged along the edge of the blue tide, heads and tails tilted up.

Near the high-water line, tough domes of sea kale push up through pure flint pebbles—waxy glaucous leaves like crumpled cabbage, their roots driving twelve feet down to find fresh moisture beneath the salt gravel. Spent half an hour skimming flat grey siltstones across the lagoon behind the bar: nine skips on an oval slate!

Crambe maritima (Sea Kale) thrives directly in bare shingle.

14th September — Closing Reflections

Sitting on the upside-down dory as the light drains out into peach and cold amber. Five things to remember from the saltmarsh:

1. The tide returns faster than a man can walk across the mud channels; always keep an eye on the gutter behind your back.

2. Carry soft chalk to mark the flint cairns when the sea-fret rolls in and erases the horizon.

3. Arthur's rule: older coins always settle where heavy shingle gives way to black anaerobic silt.

4. Sea lavender keeps its amethyst colour for years if pressed away from sea-damp.

5. The cry of the curlew at nightfall is the loneliest and most beautiful sound in England.
